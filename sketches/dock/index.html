The dock, state by state

Every state the dock can be in, each in a frame of its own. Nothing here runs. Every file is a picture, drawn before it is built, so that a state is argued about once rather than twice.

The library draws three things and no more: a window's tab, the gap between two windows, and the window under the cursor and the drop regions that exist only while a window is being moved. Everything inside a window belongs to whoever is using the library. The look is borrowed from hl to have something to draw with — it is a role in design/theme.css away from being something else.

Idle

#idle

Three windows, no overlap, and a gap between every pair. The tree is a row holding a column and the inspector; the column holds the canvas and the timeline. The axis alternates with depth, so nothing in the middle is a wrapper that exists only to turn a corner.

Two of these windows are flex and two are fixed. The canvas and the column take what is left over; the inspector claims 300px and the timeline claims 120px, and resizing the browser window does not change either of those numbers. That is the whole reason fixed exists — a column of readouts wants to be the width it needs, not a share of whatever the screen happens to be.

A window's title bar is a tab, even when it is the only one. There is no second way of drawing a window that happens to be alone, and no handle hidden inside it: the whole tab moves the window, and the whole tab picks it. One target, so there is nothing to aim at.

The mark before the name is the host's. The library has no opinion about what a window is, so it leaves room for one and draws nothing when there is none.

Resizing

#resize

The gap is the control. There is no separate handle and no hit area wider than what you can see: five pixels of the ground showing through, with a hairline down the middle that goes black while you are on it.

Every window carries a minimum in logical pixels, and a drag stops when it reaches one rather than pushing the next window along. Dragging the gap never changes which windows exist or where they sit in the tree — that is the other gesture, and keeping them apart is why a window is moved by its tab and never by a gap.

Not decided: what the dock does when the surface is smaller than the sum of the minimums. The assumption drawn into these pictures is that the minimums win and the dock overflows, because the alternative is windows crushed to a title bar and a hairline, which looks like a bug.

Where a drop lands

#drops

A window being moved has to land somewhere definite, and the user has to be able to tell where that is before letting go. So the body of a window is cut into four bands and a middle: the bands are where the nearest edge wins, and the middle is where the window joins the stack rather than taking a side of it. A tenth off each side leaves four fifths of the window in the middle, which is as it should be: sharing a slot is the ordinary thing to want, and taking a side of one is the aimed-at thing.

All five are drawn at once, and the one under the pointer is darker than the rest. Showing only the chosen one makes every move of the pointer a guess about where the boundaries are; showing the whole division answers that once, and the dark one answers what would happen.

Which band is decided by comparing the distances as fractions of the width and the height, which cuts the window along its diagonals — so a wide short window behaves like a tall narrow one. Taking the same fraction off both axes is what makes the four bands meet the middle exactly at its corners, with nothing left over and nothing overlapping.

The bands stop at the body, because a window's tabs are its order: a window carried over them lands between two of them rather than in the window under them, which is the only way a stack can be rearranged. A slot with one tab has no order to change, so a drop on its title bar joins it — and its own title bar does not slide along it: nothing about it changes until the pointer leaves the strip, and then the window lifts. From then on it stays in the air until it is put down, back over its own slot as much as anywhere — that slot is empty now, and an empty strip has nothing to stick to.

A window's tabs are a region of their own, offered with its bands and its middle so that they read as somewhere to go. Like any region, they are the darker one once the pointer is on them — and the tabs part as well, to leave the hole the window would go in.

The tabs are looked at before the dock's own edge, which means the edge cannot be reached along the top of a slot. A band nobody can see must not swallow a control everybody can.

There is one more target, and it is not a window's band: the dock's outer edge (#outer). A gap is not one: the band of the window on either side of it already lands there, so a strip along the line would be a second way of saying the same thing. Three kinds of target in total, which is as many as can be told apart without a legend.

Moving — over a window's edge

#edge

The inspector has been picked up by its tab and the pointer is in the canvas's left band. What is drawn is every region the canvas offers, with the one under the pointer darker — the regions the pointer has to be in, and not the slots the window would end up with.

Drawing the region rather than the result was a decision. The slot is the more informative of the two, but it is also the one you can work out by looking: it is where the window goes. What is hard to guess is how far the pointer has to move for the answer to change, and a shape with an edge you can see answers that without being read.

Six decisions are argued here.

The window follows its tab, and the slot it left is empty — the ground a gap shows, with no strip across it, since nothing is there. It is the window itself and not a picture of it, so there is never a second copy on screen, and whatever the host built inside it is still there when it lands. The layout does not close up behind it: a dock that reflows the moment you pick something up moves the target you were aiming at out from under your cursor. The regions are drawn under the window rather than over it — what aims is the pointer, and the pointer is on the tab.

One line goes round the tab and the window together, and none runs between them: the tab is still a piece of the window, folded up. Over a slot's strip the tab reaches over the line above it, as it does at the ends, so its top is that line and not a second one beside it. At the dock's own edge that line is whatever the host drew there, and the dock clips the tab's.

A window lifted out of a stack leaves the rest of the stack behind, and the tab that slides into its place shows, the same one that would show once it is put down. But only once its tab has left the strip: a tab being carried along its own strip is being put in order, not moved, and the window stays where it is while that happens.

What the window will get is an equal share of whatever group it lands in. Landing in a group of two gives it a third of that group; splitting one window gives it half. One rule, and it is the same rule the outer edge follows. A drop beside a window whose group already runs that way joins that group rather than nesting a new one inside it: a group whose axis matches its parent's is flattened into it.

Two things move, at two speeds. The regions fade, and the dark one moves between them by fading as well — they appear and disappear under a pointer that is already moving, so a fifth of a second is what keeps that from reading as a flicker. Once the pointer lets go, the windows settle into where they now belong rather than appearing there, and that is the slow one. But only then: a gap being dragged and a browser window being resized are continuous, and a window easing along behind either of those reads as lag rather than as motion.

What settles is every slot that was there before: the space the window left closes up and the slot it lands beside makes room, both at once, from the rectangles they had to the ones they have. A slot is known by a name of its own rather than by the windows in it, so a slot a window joins is still that slot, and the one made for the window is new. Each slot carries its own edge, drawn in the gap around it, so a slot easing into the ground the window left still has one; at rest it is the same pixel as the gap's line.

The window put down eases in from where it was in the air, at the same time and above the windows making room for it: its window from where it hung, its tab from where the pointer let go of it, and a slot made for it from around the two. None of them has a rectangle in the old layout, so where they were the moment before is kept for them to start from. A window let go of nowhere goes back the same way, and so does a tab let go of along its own strip.

Not settled: the curve. Everything eases the same way over the same three seconds, which is long on purpose while the motion is being looked at.

A press is not yet a move. A tab is picked the moment it is pressed, and has to travel a few pixels before anything lifts — so a press that goes nowhere has brought a window forward and done nothing else, and a twitch does not rearrange the screen. The threshold is a distance and not a duration, which is why nothing in the library reads a clock.

Moving — over the dock's outer edge

#outer

The pointer is in the strip along the dock's left edge, outside every window, so the window lands at the root: a full-height column down the left of everything.

This target has to exist, because it is the only way to get back a window that spans the whole height. Dropping on the canvas's left edge would put the window beside the canvas and above the timeline; nothing you can do inside a window gives you the outer column.

The row the inspector leaves is holding only the column, so it collapses into the column: a group with one child is not a group. Without that rule a week of dragging leaves a tree of wrappers nobody can see and every resize has to walk through.

Not settled: how wide the outer strip is. It has to be reachable without leaving the dock and narrow enough that a drop meant for the window beside it does not land at the root. It is 16px, here and in the library, which is a guess that has not been argued with yet.

A drop that lands nowhere

#nowhere

The pointer has left the dock. There is no preview, and that is the whole message: let go here and nothing happens — the window goes back to the slot it was lifted from, which was kept empty for it.

The same picture covers a window dropped on its own slot, and escape, and the browser taking the pointer away mid-drag. All four are one event — the gesture ends having decided nothing — so there is no fifth case where a window is in the air with nowhere to be.

The window in the air is clipped by the dock rather than drawn over the host's own chrome. Alone, and empty

#alone

One window has no gaps, because a gap is a thing between two windows and not a border. It keeps its tab: the tab is how it will be moved once there is somewhere to move it to, and a bar that comes and goes with the window count is a bar whose height nobody can lay anything out against.
